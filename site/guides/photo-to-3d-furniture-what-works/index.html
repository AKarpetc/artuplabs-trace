Photo to 3D for furniture: what works and what fails

Generating a 3D model from one product photo works well for most chairs, sofas, tables and lamps. Here is where it fails — glass, thin legs, detailed fabric, reflections — and how to shoot a photo that gives a good model.

Published 2026-10-08 · Last checked 2026-10-08

What the generator has to guess

A single product photo shows one side of the item. The generator builds the full shape and texture from that one view, so it has to infer the back, the underside and the thickness of every part. Most furniture is symmetric and made of solid parts, which is why it works well. Things that don't follow that pattern are where it struggles.

Where photo → 3D fails

These limits come from how single-photo generation works; we haven't measured failure rates for each one.

  • Glass and mirrors. Transparent or mirrored surfaces have no visible colour of their own. Glass tabletops and cabinet doors come out opaque, tinted or smeared.
  • Very thin legs, wire frames, rattan and cane. Thin parts are a few pixels wide in the photo. They can come out thicker, bent, merged with each other, or with gaps filled in.
  • Detailed fabric. Bouclé loops, fine weave, quilting and pleats become a printed texture on a smooth surface, not real 3D detail. From a distance this looks right; close up it looks flat.
  • Glossy and reflective finishes. Lacquer, chrome and polished stone reflect the room around them; those reflections get baked into the colour.
  • Hidden parts. What the photo can't see — the back of a sofa, the inside of a drawer — is a plausible guess, not the real product.
  • Size. A photo has no scale. The model's proportions follow the photo, but its real size has to be set from the product's dimensions before AR, otherwise a chair may appear doll-sized or huge.

Example

A carved chair with turned legs from one museum photo. Look at the legs and the back when you rotate it: the front view is close to the photo, the back is inferred.

How to shoot a photo that works

  1. One object in the frame. Remove the side table, plant, cushions that aren't part of the product, and people.
  2. Plain background. A white or evenly coloured wall, or a studio cut-out on white.
  3. The whole item. Nothing cropped at the edges; legs fully visible down to the floor.
  4. A three-quarter angle. Front and one side visible gives more shape than a straight front view.
  5. Soft, even light. No hard shadows across the item, no bright reflections.
  6. Sharp and large enough. A sharp photo where the item fills most of the frame; your existing catalog photo is usually fine.

Then check the result before it goes live: rotate the model, look at the legs and the back, and regenerate or reject it if it isn't right. The app has an accept / regenerate / reject step for this reason.
